Dear Editor,

I submit the Original Article “Retinal foundation-model versus ImageNet
pretraining for diabetic retinopathy grading across datasets: how much
of the external performance drop reflects the reference standard?” for
consideration in International Ophthalmology.

Automated diabetic retinopathy (DR) grading performs well on the data it
is developed on but loses accuracy when the camera, population or
grading protocol changes. Retinal foundation models such as RETFound are
often expected to solve this problem. This study tests that expectation
directly. It also asks a question that matters to every ophthalmologist
who reads an AI validation paper: how much of an “external performance
drop” is model failure, and how much reflects a different case mix or a
different reference standard?

Models were developed on APTOS 2019 and evaluated once, unchanged, on
Messidor-2 (adjudicated specialist grades) and the official IDRiD
release. The pre-specified primary analysis, with multiplicity control,
found that fully fine-tuned RETFound degraded neither less nor
detectably more than an ImageNet-pretrained EfficientNet-B3. The study
then shows that:

-   most of the Messidor-2 drop disappears once case mix is matched and
    no/mild DR are merged, and the two external datasets fail in
    opposite directions at that boundary, which points to differences in
    the reference standard rather than to model fragility;
-   RETFound’s apparent calibration advantage is reproduced by label
    smoothing alone, a training-recipe effect rather than a benefit of
    retinal pretraining;
-   confidence-based referral halved missed referable DR on one external
    dataset but not the other, and simple confidence was as informative
    as more elaborate uncertainty scores;
-   tested quantitatively against expert lesion masks, saliency maps
    localised lesions only weakly, so they should not be read as proof
    of interpretability.

The study is therefore an evaluation of cross-dataset reliability, not a
proposal for another classifier. It is reported following TRIPOD+AI. It
includes an audit that found 5.9% of labels in a widely shared IDRiD
mirror differed from the official release, and it provides per-image
predictions for every model. The work adds to recent articles in this
journal on deep learning for DR and retinal imaging by emphasising
external testing, confidence intervals and quantitative evaluation of
uncertainty and explainability.

I confirm that this manuscript is original, has not been published
previously, and is not under consideration by any other journal. It has
